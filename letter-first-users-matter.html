Why Your First Users Matter More Than Your First Investors

Before you pitch a big vision, find a few real people who need what you are building. Their feedback can shape a better business than a room full of applause.

By E-Cell VSBEC • 29 September 2026 • 6 min read

Imagine you have built a prototype for a campus app. Your slides look sharp, your logo is ready, and your pitch explains a huge opportunity. But one question remains: has anyone actually used it to solve a real problem?

For early founders, this question matters more than whether an investor likes the presentation. Investors can provide capital, connections, and guidance. But your first users show you whether your product is useful outside your imagination.

Your first users are not just an audience. They are your earliest source of evidence.

1. A pitch can explain the vision. Users test the reality.

A pitch is a story about what could happen. A user interacting with your product gives you evidence about what is happening now. They may ignore the feature you thought was essential, get confused by a simple screen, or ask for something you never considered.

That is not a failure of the idea. It is information you can use before investing more time and money.

2. Your first five users can reveal patterns

You do not need hundreds of sign-ups to begin learning. Start with five people who genuinely experience the problem you want to solve. Ask them to try the simplest version you have, then observe what they do rather than only listening to what they say.

01 / Observe

Where do they hesitate?

Confusion, repeated questions, and workarounds can reveal friction you missed.

02 / Ask

What is still difficult?

Ask what they do today and which part takes the most time or effort.

03 / Compare

What repeats?

If several people struggle with the same thing, investigate that pattern.

04 / Improve

What should change?

Make one useful change, then check whether it actually helps.

Five users will not prove that an entire market wants your product. They can, however, expose obvious problems and help you decide what to test next.

3. Do not mistake compliments for demand

People are often kind when you show them a project. They may say, “That is a great idea,” because they want to encourage you. Encouragement is valuable, but it is not the same as demand.

Look for actions: Do they try the product? Do they return to it? Do they recommend it to someone with the same problem? Would they change an existing habit, spend time, or pay for a better solution?

Better questions to ask early users

  • “How do you handle this problem today?”
  • “What is the most frustrating part of that process?”
  • “Can you show me the last time this happened?”
  • “What would make you use this again?”

Avoid asking only, “Would you use my app?” It invites a polite yes. Questions about real past behaviour usually lead to more useful conversations.

4. Build a small feedback loop

Early product development does not have to be complicated. Keep the cycle short: share a basic version, watch someone use it, identify one important problem, improve the product, and test again.

Day 1–2

Find the problem. Talk to people who experience it and write down their current workaround.

Day 3–5

Make a simple prototype. Build only enough to test the most important assumption.

Day 6–7

Watch users try it. Let them explore without guiding every click.

Next week

Improve and repeat. Share what changed and what you still need to learn.

5. When should you think about investors?

Funding can be important, especially when a business needs equipment, specialised talent, inventory, or faster expansion. But raising money before understanding the customer can make you scale the wrong thing more quickly.

Early user evidence helps you explain what you are building, who needs it, what you have learned, and why the next stage deserves resources. You do not need to wait until you have a perfect product or a large customer base. You need a credible signal that you are solving a real problem.

For student founders, that signal might be a small group using a campus tool every week, a local business adopting an automation, or a prototype that demonstrably saves people time. Start with evidence you can collect honestly.

The takeaway

Your first users may never appear in a funding announcement. They may not have impressive titles or large networks. But they are the people who help you discover whether your product belongs in the real world.

So before spending weeks polishing a pitch deck, find five people with the problem. Listen carefully. Let their behaviour challenge your assumptions. Then build the next version with what you learned.

Do not just ask people to believe in your idea. Give them something useful enough to try.

Try this week: Choose one project idea, speak with five potential users, and write down the three most repeated problems you hear. Build your next step around what you learn — not what you assumed.
