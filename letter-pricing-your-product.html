Pricing Your Product: From Guesswork to a Clear Strategy

A price is more than a number on a checkout page. It communicates value, shapes customer expectations, and determines whether a business can sustain the work required to serve its market.

By E-Cell VSBEC • 11 October 2026 • 6 min read

Pricing is often postponed until a product is almost ready to launch. The team focuses on features, design, and marketing, then chooses a price based on a competitor or a quick guess. That approach can leave a good product struggling to cover its costs — or make a useful product seem less valuable than it is.

A stronger approach begins earlier. Price should be treated as part of the product strategy: who the product serves, what problem it solves, how much value it creates, and what it takes to deliver that value reliably.

Start with the value, not the number

Customers rarely evaluate a price in isolation. They compare it with the result they expect, the alternatives available, and the effort required to switch. A product that saves a business several hours each week may justify a different price from a product that offers a minor convenience.

This does not mean charging the highest amount a customer might accept. It means understanding the outcome your product provides and being able to explain that outcome clearly. If the value is difficult to describe, the pricing problem may actually be a product-positioning problem.

Ask this first

What meaningful outcome does the customer receive, and how does that outcome compare with the cost of using the product?

Understand the three main pricing approaches

Cost-based pricing

Calculate the cost of delivering the product and add a margin. This helps protect sustainability, but it does not reveal what customers value.

Competitor-based pricing

Compare similar offers in the market. Useful for context, but competitors may serve different customers or have different costs.

Value-based pricing

Set a price around the value customers perceive and receive. This requires customer research and a clear understanding of outcomes.

A balanced decision

Use costs to understand your floor, competitors to understand the landscape, and customer value to guide the offer.

No single method answers every question. Costs matter because a business cannot operate sustainably while losing money on every sale. Competitors matter because customers have alternatives. Customer value matters because price must make sense to the people you want to serve.

Know your costs before setting a price

List the costs involved in making, selling, and supporting the product. Depending on the business, these may include materials, payment fees, hosting, packaging, delivery, customer support, labour, and marketing. Separate costs that change with each sale from fixed costs that remain even when sales are low.

Then estimate how many sales you need to cover those costs. The calculation will not predict the future perfectly, but it will make the assumptions visible. If the price only works when sales are unrealistically high, revisit the cost structure, the target customer, or the offer itself.

Remember that revenue is not profit. Discounts, refunds, taxes, transaction fees, and ongoing support can significantly affect what remains after a sale.

Choose a pricing model that fits the product

The structure of the price matters as much as the amount. A one-time purchase may suit a tool that delivers a single, lasting outcome. A subscription may suit a service that provides continuing value. Usage-based pricing can work when customer value and delivery costs increase with consumption.

  • One-time payment: simple to understand, but future development must be funded by new sales or other revenue.
  • Subscription: can support ongoing service and predictable revenue, but customers need a reason to keep paying.
  • Usage-based: aligns payment with consumption, but bills should remain understandable and predictable.
  • Tiered plans: allow different customer groups to choose the level of service they need, provided the differences are clear.

Avoid adding tiers just to make a pricing page look sophisticated. Each option should solve a distinct customer need without making the choice unnecessarily confusing.

Test pricing with real customer evidence

Research and calculations can narrow the options, but real customer behaviour is still important. Explain the offer and its price to people in your target market. Ask what they compare it with, what feels unclear, and what would need to be true for the purchase to make sense.

Be careful with questions such as “Would you buy this?” People may give supportive answers without intending to purchase. Stronger evidence includes a paid pilot, a signed commitment, a renewal, or a clear decision to choose the offer over an alternative.

When testing different prices, change one major variable at a time where practical. Track not only conversion, but also refunds, retention, support costs, and customer fit. A lower price that attracts many unsuitable customers may be less valuable than a higher price that supports a healthier, more sustainable business.

A practical pricing checklist

01

Define the customer and outcome

Be specific about who buys the product and which problem or result matters most to them.

02

Calculate the cost structure

Understand delivery costs, fixed expenses, margins, and the sales volume required to sustain the business.

03

Compare the alternatives

Review competitors and substitutes, including the option of doing nothing or using an existing manual process.

04

Choose a clear initial offer

Make the price, included features, limits, and terms easy to understand.

05

Review evidence and adjust

Watch purchase behaviour, retention, profitability, and customer feedback. Change the price when the evidence supports a change.

Pricing is a decision you keep learning from

There is rarely a perfect price that can be discovered through research alone. Markets change, costs shift, customer expectations evolve, and the product itself improves. A sound pricing strategy makes assumptions explicit and creates a way to test them responsibly.

Do not compete on being the cheapest by default. Focus on delivering a clear outcome, explaining why it matters, and charging an amount that customers can justify and your business can sustain.

The takeaway

Good pricing sits at the intersection of customer value, business costs, and market context. Start with the problem you solve, understand the economics of delivering the solution, and use real customer behaviour to refine the offer.

The objective is not simply to choose a number that attracts buyers. It is to create a fair, understandable exchange that supports lasting value for both the customer and the business.
